# Landing page reference

A landing page has one job: move a visitor to one action. Every section either builds the case for that action or removes a doubt about it.

## Before designing

Get these from PLAN.md, or state your assumptions in the report:

- Who the visitor is and what problem they have.
- The single action the page wants (the conversion goal).
- The main reason to choose this product or service over alternatives.
- What proof exists: customers, numbers, reviews, photos of real work.

## Section order

Use this as the default and remove what the project has no content for. Don't invent proof to fill a section.

1. **Header:** logo, a few anchor links, and the primary action.
2. **Hero:** what it is, who it's for, and the primary action. Visible without scrolling.
3. **Proof strip:** customer logos, a rating, or one strong number.
4. **Problem or benefits:** three to four benefits, each with a short heading and one or two sentences.
5. **How it works:** three or four numbered steps.
6. **Showcase:** product photos, screenshots, or examples of real work.
7. **Testimonials:** real quotes with a name and context.
8. **Pricing or offer:** if it applies, with the recommended option highlighted.
9. **FAQ:** the real objections, answered directly.
10. **Final call to action:** repeat the offer and the primary action.
11. **Footer:** contact details, legal links, secondary links.

## Hero

- Headline: the main benefit in about ten words or fewer. A visitor should understand the offer in five seconds.
- Subheadline: one or two sentences that add who it's for or how it works.
- One primary button. A secondary link ("See examples") is fine; a second equal button is not.
- A visual that shows the real product or result, not a generic stock photo.
- Keep the headline, subheadline, and button together and above the fold on a phone.

## Calls to action

- One conversion goal for the whole page. Every primary button leads to it and uses the same label.
- Repeat the primary action after the hero, after the proof or pricing, and at the end.
- The label states the outcome ("Get a free quote"). Add a short reassurance under it when it helps ("No credit card needed", "Reply within one business day").
- The button is the most visually prominent element in its section, with space around it.

## Copy

- Write to "you", about the visitor's outcome, before talking about the company.
- Turn features into benefits: say what the feature lets the visitor do.
- Be specific. Numbers, timeframes, and concrete nouns beat adjectives such as "best" or "innovative".
- One idea per section, with a heading that works even if it's the only thing read. Most visitors scan headings.
- Keep paragraphs to two or three lines. Use lists for three or more parallel points.
- Never invent testimonials, customer names, statistics, or guarantees. If real ones aren't provided, leave a clearly marked placeholder and list it in the report.

## Lead forms

- Put the form on the page or one click away, and ask for the minimum (often name, email, and one message field).
- Say what happens after submitting and how soon.
- Show a confirmation state, and say so if a confirmation email is sent.
- Link the privacy policy near the submit button when collecting personal data.

## Visual rhythm

- Alternate section backgrounds (background, then surface) to separate sections without lines.
- Vary layouts so the page doesn't feel like one repeated block: centered text, then image beside text, then a grid.
- Keep the same container width, section spacing, and heading style from top to bottom.
- Use the accent color sparingly so buttons stand out wherever they appear.

## Checks before reporting

- Read only the headings and buttons from top to bottom. They should tell the whole story.
- The primary action is reachable within one screen of any scroll position, or the header keeps it visible.
- The page works with images turned off and reads well on a 375px-wide screen.
- Hand off metadata, structured data, and Core Web Vitals to the `react-nextjs` skill's SEO reference.
